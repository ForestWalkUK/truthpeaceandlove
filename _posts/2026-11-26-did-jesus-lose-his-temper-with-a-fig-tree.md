More than twenty years ago, I read in the Bible that Jesus once cursed a fig tree, because He was hungry and it had no fruit on it. I was shocked. Surely the meek and gentle Jesus would not act in such a petulant, bad-tempered way, and in front of His disciples too, set down on the public record for all the world to see! Isn’t that just the kind of impatience I know only too well in myself when things don’t go my way? It troubled me that the Bible itself seemed to record something that reflected badly on Him. I’m very pleased to tell you that the fault lay not with Jesus, nor with the Bible, but with my own understanding. If you have ever felt the same, let me show you what I came to see.

Here is the account, as Mark records it:

> “And on the morrow, when they were come from Bethany, he was hungry: And seeing a fig tree afar off having leaves, he came, if haply he might find any thing thereon: and when he came to it, he found nothing but leaves; for the time of figs was not *yet*. And Jesus answered and said unto it, No man eat fruit of thee hereafter for ever. And his disciples heard *it*.” (Mark 11:12-14)

## Look closer

His hunger was real. He is truly man as well as truly God, and He “was in all points tempted like as *we are, yet* without sin” (Hebrews 4:15). Hunger is no sin. But this is the One who twice fed thousands in the wilderness from a few loaves and fishes. When His disciples once urged Him, “Master, eat”, He answered, “My meat is to do the will of him that sent me, and to finish his work.” (John 4:31, 34). Whatever He was doing at the fig tree, He was not sulking over a missed breakfast.

Now look at the tree. “The time of figs was not *yet*”, and yet this tree was already in full leaf, as if it had fruit to offer. From a distance it promised much; close up there was “nothing but leaves”. It was all show.

Then look at where Mark places it. The tree was cursed on the way into Jerusalem. That same day the Lord drove the traders out of the temple, saying, “Is it not written, My house shall be called of all nations the house of prayer? but ye have made it a den of thieves.” (Mark 11:17). The next morning the tree was found “dried up from the roots” (Mark 11:20). The tree and the temple explain each other. The temple was in full leaf: crowds, sacrifices, the busy trade of religion. But the fruit God looked for was not there.

<details class="explainer">
<summary>How do we know that the tree and the temple explain each other? <em>(tap or click to read)</em></summary>
<p>Mark tells the story of the fig tree in two halves, with the temple in between, and this is the order in which it happened, day by day. On the evening He arrived, the Lord went into the temple and “looked round about upon all things” (Mark 11:11), then went out to Bethany. The next morning, on the way back, He came to the fig tree looking for fruit and found none (Mark 11:12-14). Then He went into the temple and drove out the traders (Mark 11:15-17). The following morning the disciples saw the tree “dried up from the roots” (Mark 11:20). He looked over the temple, then the tree, and in neither did He find the fruit He sought.</p>
<p>His words in the temple point the same way. “My house shall be called of all nations the house of prayer” is taken from Isaiah 56:7. “A den of thieves” is taken from Jeremiah, whose words in our Bible read “a den of robbers” (Jeremiah 7:11). Some six hundred years earlier, Jeremiah stood at the gate of the temple of his day and warned the people not to trust in it (Jeremiah 7:2-4). In that same message, which runs from Jeremiah 7:1 to the end of chapter 10, God said there would be “no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree, and the leaf shall fade” (Jeremiah 8:13). The prophet had already joined the temple and the fig tree, and the Lord took up his warning in deed as well as in word.</p>
<p>Mark often sets two events side by side like this, so that each throws light on the other. In chapter 5, Jairus, a ruler of the synagogue, begs the Lord to come to his dying daughter. On the way, a woman who has suffered an issue of blood for twelve years touches His garment and is healed. While the Lord stops for her, word comes: “Thy daughter is dead” (Mark 5:35). He says to Jairus, “Be not afraid, only believe.” (Mark 5:36), and raises the girl from the dead.</p>
<p>The two accounts mirror each other. The woman had been ill for twelve years, and the girl was “<em>of the age</em> of twelve years” (Mark 5:25, 42). Both are called “daughter” (Mark 5:23, 34). Under the law, touching either of them would make a person unclean: the woman because of her issue, the girl because of death (Leviticus 15:19, 25; Numbers 19:11). Yet when the woman touched Him, and when He took the girl by the hand, no uncleanness passed to Him. Instead, healing and life passed from Him (Mark 5:30, 41). And Jairus, at his darkest moment, had just heard the Lord say to the woman, “Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole” (Mark 5:34).</p>
<p>Matthew and Luke tell these events in the same order (Matthew 9:18-26; Luke 8:40-56), because that is how they happened. God “worketh all things after the counsel of his own will” (Ephesians 1:11), down to the moment a woman reached out in a crowd. The Holy Spirit then moved the Gospel writers to record the events so that we can see it (2 Peter 1:21). Not every pairing in Mark follows the order of events: in chapter 6 the death of John the Baptist is told looking back (Mark 6:16-29). But whether God ordered the events themselves or guided the writer to set them side by side, the pattern is His.</p>
<p>Matthew tells the fig tree account more briefly. He groups it by subject, putting the temple first and telling the cursing and the withering together (Matthew 21:12-22), and he says the tree withered “presently”, an old word for “at once”. Mark gives the order day by day. Both are true: they tell the same events from different sides.</p>
</details>

## A solemn warning

The fig tree was the prophets’ picture of Israel (Jeremiah 8:13), and the Lord had already told a parable about a fig tree that bore nothing year after year: “cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?” (Luke 13:7). Now, days before the cross, He acted that parable out. As Matthew Henry put it, “He made this fig-tree an example, not to the trees, but to the men of that generation.” (Concise Commentary, on Mark 11:12-18). The withered tree was God’s sentence on a religion with leaves and no fruit. Within forty years, in A.D. 70, the Romans destroyed the temple. Multitudes were slain, and many of the survivors were led away captive, just as the Lord had foretold (Luke 21:24).

But the fig tree speaks to us too. Every one of us is a tree from which God looks for fruit, and not one of us has given Him what is His due. God has told us plainly what He looks for. His law is summed up in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1-17), and “sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4): every breaking of those commandments, and every failure to keep them, in thought, word or deed. We may think our own sins are small beside the terrible crimes in the news. Yet the Lord Jesus traced murder back to anger, and adultery back to a look (Matthew 5:22, 28). And the weight of a wrong is measured by the one it is done against. To strike a stranger is a crime; to strike the king is treason. Every sin, even the ones we call “little”, is done against the infinitely holy God, and “our God *is* a consuming fire” (Hebrews 12:29). That is the terrible side of the fig tree, and we must not look away from it.

## A loving Saviour

But look at the One who spoke the sentence. Only days before, as He came near to Jerusalem, “he beheld the city, and wept over it” (Luke 19:41). He takes “no pleasure in the death of the wicked” (Ezekiel 33:11). When we are petulant, we lash out because we have not got what we wanted for ourselves. The Lord Jesus warned because He wanted something for them: “the things *which belong* unto thy peace” (Luke 19:42).

And He did far more than warn. He came to bear the curse Himself. “Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed *is* every one that hangeth on a tree” (Galatians 3:13). Within days of cursing a tree, He hung upon a tree, made a curse. He who was God manifest in the flesh bore in His soul and body the outpoured wrath of God against sin and sinners, in the place of all who would trust in Him.

This is why it matters so much that He was not bad-tempered that morning. Every sin is a debt we owe to God (Matthew 6:12), and every one of us is bankrupt, with nothing to pay (Luke 7:42). Yet even if our debt were wiped out, we would only be back to nothing, and nothing is not enough to stand before a holy God. We need a perfect record of obedience to God, every moment of the life He has given us, and not one of us has it. Had the Lord Jesus sinned even once, He would have had a debt of His own to answer for, and no perfect record to give. But He never did. Conceived by the Holy Ghost, He never inherited Adam’s fallen nature. He “did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth” (1 Peter 2:22). On the cross He paid in full the debt of all who would trust in Him. And His own perfect obedience, of infinite worth because He is the Son of God, is put to their account as well (2 Corinthians 5:21). So the moment you repent and believe, you pass once and for all from infinite debt to infinite credit, and there you stand for ever, because “though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich” (2 Corinthians 8:9).

<details class="explainer">
<summary>If that is so, why do Christians still ask God every day to forgive them? <em>(tap or click to read)</em></summary>
<p>Because God is both their Judge and their Father. As Judge, He justifies a sinner once and for all, the moment they believe: all their sins are pardoned, and no sin they commit afterwards can ever bring them back into condemnation. “<em>There is</em> therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus” (Romans 8:1), “For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified” (Hebrews 10:14).</p>
<p>But those whom the Judge has pardoned He also takes into His family as His children, and children can still grieve their Father. When believers sin, they do not fall back under God’s wrath, but they do fall under their Father’s displeasure. He may correct them, “For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth” (Hebrews 12:6), and for a time they may lose the sense of His smile and the joy of His presence, until they come back to Him humbled and confess their sin. So the Lord Jesus taught His disciples to pray every day, “And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors” (Matthew 6:12). As the Puritan Thomas Watson put it in his exposition of the Lord’s Prayer, “We sin daily, and must ask for daily pardon as well as for daily bread.” And the promise still stands: “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us <em>our</em> sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9).</p>
<p>King David shows the difference. After his great sin he did not pray to be saved all over again, but “Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation” (Psalm 51:12). The believer’s daily prayer for forgiveness is not a fresh plea to be justified. It is a child coming home to a Father who never stopped being their Father.</p>
<p>This comfort belongs only to those who truly believe. Not everyone who professes faith has it. Some, like Simon the sorcerer, are baptised and keep company with God’s people for a time, yet were never His children (Acts 8:13, 21): “They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would <em>no doubt</em> have continued with us” (1 John 2:19). God’s children are known by this: when they sin, they come back to their Father, grieved and humbled. Anyone who can go on in sin without sorrow, and never return, has good reason to fear that they have never been His child at all (Hebrews 12:8). “Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith” (2 Corinthians 13:5).</p>
<p>The Westminster Confession of Faith puts it this way: “God doth continue to forgive the sins of those that are justified; and, although they can never fall from the state of justification, yet they may, by their sins, fall under God’s fatherly displeasure, and not have the light of his countenance restored unto them, until they humble themselves, confess their sins, beg pardon, and renew their faith and repentance.” (Chapter 11, section 5)</p>
</details>

## “Have faith in God”

The next morning Peter saw the tree and cried, “Master, behold, the fig tree which thou cursedst is withered away.” (Mark 11:21). Jesus answered, “Have faith in God.” (Mark 11:22). He spoke of faith that could remove a mountain and cast it into the sea, and then said: “Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive *them*, and ye shall have *them*.” (Mark 11:24).

Now we can see why the promise comes at this very spot. The withered tree was proof that what the Son of God says comes to pass: one sentence, and by morning it was dead from the roots. That is the God in whom we are called to trust.

It also shows what kind of prayer He means. The temple had failed to be a house of prayer; now He was teaching His own to pray. Prayer that is in line with God and His kingdom, asked truly for His sake, shall be answered, however impossible it looks to man. Sometimes God gives the very thing that was asked. Where He does not, He answers the deepest heart of the prayer, giving something better. The disciples were still hoping for an earthly kingdom (Acts 1:6). But God had a far greater mountain to remove: our sin, and the curse it deserves, taken away at the cross.

And notice His very next words: “And when ye stand praying, forgive, if ye have ought against any: that your Father also which is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses.” (Mark 11:25). The same Lord who cursed the fruitless tree tells His people, when they pray, to lay down every grudge. This does not mean pretending a wrong was never done, or trusting again someone who has not repented: “If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him.” (Luke 17:3). Nor does it mean keeping a crime hidden. God has appointed the civil authorities to punish wrongdoing (Romans 13:4), so reporting a crime and letting justice take its course is not revenge. But it does mean letting go of all bitterness and every wish for revenge, leaving that to God (Romans 12:19), and standing ready to forgive fully the moment they repent. Faith in God and a forgiving heart: that is the fruit of those who have themselves been forgiven so much. (I have written more on this promise in [“Believing Prayer: A Catechism on Mark 11:24”]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-10-18-believing-prayer-catechism-mark-11-24 %}).)

## Back where I began

So, when Jesus cursed that fig tree, was He being petulant because He was hungry? No, no, no. He was lovingly warning of the judgement that must fall on all who reject their Maker, and weeping as He warned. And He was pointing to the cross. By our treason against God, every one of us stands beneath the sword of His justice. At the cross that sword fell upon the Lord Jesus, in the place of all who would trust in Him: “Awake, O sword, against my shepherd … smite the shepherd” (Zechariah 13:7). Whoever believes on Him is out from under it for ever. Whoever will not believe remains beneath it, for “he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him” (John 3:36). Far from being a fit of temper, what the Lord did that morning was a noble and glorious act of the Most High: infinitely loving, and yet dreadfully terrible. As the apostle Paul says, “Behold therefore the goodness and severity of God” (Romans 11:22).

The One who cursed the fig tree, and then hung upon a tree, rose again and is alive today: “*I am* he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore” (Revelation 1:18). He still says, “Come unto me, all *ye* that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.” (Matthew 11:28), and “him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out.” (John 6:37).

“For who in the heaven can be compared unto the LORD? *who* among the sons of the mighty can be likened unto the LORD?” (Psalm 89:6)
