> **“I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life.”**
>
> — John 8:12

Halloween is everywhere at this time of year: decorations, costumes, parties. Most people join in for fun and mean no harm, and I understand why such evenings appeal. In a world of constant bad news, with families and communities breaking down, any reason to get together seems welcome. There is real bonding in facing fears and laughing at them together as the dark nights draw in, with black humour and corny, tongue-in-cheek horror. But it’s worth asking what the night actually is.

---

## Where Halloween came from

The name means “All Hallows’ Even”, the eve of All Saints’ Day, a feast set by the Church of Rome in the Middle Ages. Its customs grew up around praying for the dead, in the belief that such prayers could help their souls on into heaven, which the Bible shows to be in vain. “Trick or treat” comes from “souling”, when the poor went door to door begging cakes in return for prayers for the dead. Behind that lies an older pagan festival at the start of winter, long associated with spirits and fortune-telling. At the Reformation, Scotland abolished such days because none of it came from the Bible.

Some will say, “We don’t care about the history; it’s just fun now.” But the question is not only where Halloween came from, but what it now celebrates: violence, death, witchcraft and fear, turned into entertainment. And enjoying something does not make it good. The Bible speaks of “the pleasures of sin for a season” (Hebrews 11:25), and warns, “There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof *are* the ways of death” (Proverbs 14:12). In the end, what matters is not how a thing feels to us, but how God sees it.

## Why the Bible should decide

But why believe that, and why should the Bible decide the matter? Because from beginning to end it claims to be the Word of God. “Thus saith the LORD” runs through it hundreds of times, and it declares that “All scripture *is* given by inspiration of God” (2 Timothy 3:16). It also proves that claim in a way no other book can: by foretelling the future. God Himself sets this test: “*I am* God, and *there is* none like me, Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times *the things* that are not *yet* done” (Isaiah 46:9-10). For example, centuries in advance, the prophets foretold that the Saviour would be born in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2); that He would be despised and rejected, wounded for the sins of others, and laid in a rich man’s grave (Isaiah 53); and that men would cast lots for His clothing (Psalm 22:18). All of this was fulfilled in Jesus Christ.

Isaiah and Micah both prophesied around 700 B.C., some seven centuries before Christ. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls is a complete copy of Isaiah, made over a century before Jesus was born. It agrees closely with the Bibles we read today, showing both that these words were written beforehand and that they have been faithfully preserved. Read it for yourself (the Gospel of John is a good place to begin), and see whether it speaks as no other book does.

## Playing with fear

Why, though, do so many enjoy being frightened? Perhaps a fright that seems harmless lets people brush against the thing they fear most of all, death, while feeling safe. But sadly, our deadly enemy the devil knows this very well, and uses it to draw people, especially children and teenagers, further into his soul-destroying ways. There are real, dark and evil spiritual forces at work in our world, and our own neighbourhoods are no exception.

Some would say that cartoon ghosts and jokey witches simply make fun of evil. But those who practise witchcraft today do not treat this night as a joke, and the Church of Satan names it among its major festivals. For modern witches and pagans it is one of the most sacred nights of the year, and RE:Online, a guide to world religions for teachers in UK schools, says that at this time “Communicating with ancestors and departed loved ones is easy”.[^1] Yet the Bible records only one occasion when a departed soul appeared to someone consulting a medium: Samuel, to King Saul (1 Samuel 28). Commentators differ on whether it was Samuel himself or an evil spirit impersonating him, but either way Saul died for “asking *counsel* of *one that had* a familiar spirit” (1 Chronicles 10:13). And when the rich man in the Lord’s parable begged that Lazarus be sent back from the dead to warn his brothers, the answer was, “They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them” (Luke 16:29). Scripture speaks far more of “familiar spirits” (Leviticus 19:31): fallen angels, malevolent and intent on our eternal destruction, who mislead and lie to those who seek their counsel, for “Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14). If those who misguidedly practise these things take them so seriously, is it wise for anyone to treat them lightly? How can we know where harmless fun ends? Many who have “played” with a Ouija board tell of being badly frightened, and even some practising witches warn others never to touch one. God has given the answer: “Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. Abstain from all appearance of evil” (1 Thessalonians 5:21-22).

## God is not against joy

None of this means God is against enjoyment. He is not anti-pleasure. He is the source of everlasting pleasure: “in thy presence *is* fulness of joy; at thy right hand *there are* pleasures for evermore” (Psalm 16:11). Nor is He against love, laughter or having a good time. The Bible tells us there is “a time to laugh” (Ecclesiastes 3:4), and in the Lord’s parable of the prodigal son, the son’s homecoming was celebrated with music and dancing (Luke 15:25). And of those who hear, believe and trust in Him, He promises, “The LORD thy God in the midst of thee *is* mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing” (Zephaniah 3:17).

We may, and should, hold celebrations on proper occasions. But surely our celebrations should never lead children towards the very things God warns us against.

## The good news

Death is no game: “it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27). God, our faithful Creator, forbids dealings with spirits and fortune-telling (Deuteronomy 18:10-12) because these things belong to a darkness that holds every one of us by nature. God made the first man in His own image, upright and holy (Genesis 1:27; Ecclesiastes 7:29). But he listened to the devil and fell, and the whole human race fell with him; that is why the world is so full of bad news. Our very nature is now corrupted, so that we sin readily and willingly. Jesus said, “Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin” (John 8:34), and the Bible describes all who are without Christ as caught in “the snare of the devil, who are taken captive by him at his will” (2 Timothy 2:26). But God has the best possible news for us (the very word “gospel” means “good news”). In His mercy, grace and love, He has not left us there. He seeks to rescue us from sin, from Satan, and from the judgment to come.

That is why Jesus Christ came, “that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil; And deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage” (Hebrews 2:14-15). He died for sinners and rose again. He says, “I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life” (John 8:12).

Turn from your sin and trust in Christ, for He promises, “him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out” (John 6:37). “Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved” (Acts 16:31).

Halloween gives people a reason to gather, but God offers something far better: peace with Him through Jesus Christ, and the joys of His presence for ever.
