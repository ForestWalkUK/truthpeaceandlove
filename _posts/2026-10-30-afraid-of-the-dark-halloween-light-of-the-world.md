## In brief

Halloween is everywhere at this time of year. For many it means a little witch and a dinosaur at the door, a garden full of fog and cobwebs, a fundraiser for a good cause, a party with friends, a horror film under a blanket, or simply candles and warmth as the dark nights draw in. Most people join in for fun and mean no harm, and much of what they are looking for is good: memories with their children, a reason to gather, a place to belong, comfort against the dark. But can this night really give people what they are looking for, and what is it teaching them while they enjoy it?

The name means “All Hallows’ Even”, the eve of a feast set by the Church of Rome in the Middle Ages, whose customs grew up around praying for the dead, which the Bible shows to be in vain. Behind it lies an older pagan festival associated with spirits and fortune-telling. Many will say, “We don’t care about the history; it’s just fun now.” Yet whatever people intend, the night’s imagery carries its own message: that the dead may wander among us, and that witchcraft and death are things to play with. In the end, what matters is not how a thing feels to us, but how God sees it.

How can we know how God sees it? Through the Bible, which among many evidences proves itself to be His Word by foretelling the future. Centuries in advance, the prophets foretold that the Saviour would be born in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), and that He would be wounded for the sins of others (Isaiah 53). All of this was fulfilled in Jesus Christ.

The Bible tells us that at death the soul goes at once to heaven or to hell (Luke 16:22-23; Luke 23:43); the dead do not linger among us. The spirits that answer those who seek them, whether through a medium or a Ouija board, are fallen angels, for “Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14). Many who were once deep in witchcraft, and have since been set free by Christ, warn that Halloween is an open door into that darkness. Misrepresenting the spirit world grieves God, and leads people away from the saving truth of His Word into the lies of the devil, who “is a liar, and the father of it” (John 8:44). And his lies come sugar-coated, like the sweets handed out at the door. Meanwhile, for modern witches and pagans this is one of the most sacred nights of the year. If they take it so seriously, is it wise for anyone to treat it lightly? “Abstain from all appearance of evil” (1 Thessalonians 5:22).

Yet “God is love” (1 John 4:16); He is the fountain of all true joy and the Creator of laughter itself: “in thy presence *is* fulness of joy; at thy right hand *there are* pleasures for evermore” (Psalm 16:11). But death is no game: “it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27). However kind or decent we may be, we have all broken God’s law, summarised in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1-17), for “sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4). By nature we are all sinners, and all who are without Christ are held in “the snare of the devil” (2 Timothy 2:26). No good deeds of ours can pay for our sins. God is marvellously patient with sinners, but His patience must never be presumed upon (Romans 2:4). A judge who let a guilty man walk free would not be a good judge, and God is the perfectly good Judge (Genesis 18:25): He must and will punish sin. And since every sin is committed against the infinitely great and holy God, even one sin is infinitely serious, so that His just sentence on all who die in their sins is eternal damnation (Matthew 25:46).

The good news is that Jesus Christ came “that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil; And deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage” (Hebrews 2:14-15). He died for sinners, bearing in the place of all who trust in Him the punishment their sins deserve, and rose again. He welcomes those whom others push away, and He alone can save: “I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me” (John 14:6). Salvation is not earned, for “the wages of sin *is* death; but the gift of God *is* eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord” (Romans 6:23).

Turn from your sin and trust in Christ, for “now *is* the day of salvation” (2 Corinthians 6:2), and He promises, “him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out” (John 6:37). “Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved” (Acts 16:31).

Halloween offers a pumpkin’s flickering light against the dark for a single night. God offers the joys of His presence for ever, in a world of which it is written, “there shall be no night there; and they need no candle, neither light of the sun; for the Lord God giveth them light” (Revelation 22:5).

## The full article

Halloween is everywhere at this time of year: pumpkins on doorsteps, costumes in the shops, invitations to parties. People love it for all sorts of reasons, and many of them are touching. A mother loves seeing her children’s faces light up at a neighbour’s door, knowing they are only little once. A father spends weeks turning his garden into a haunted house, because it brings the whole street out and raises money for a good cause. Teenagers and students love a night when they can be someone else and nobody judges them. Some simply love being scared, and the thrill of a fright they know they will survive. Others love the cosiness of candles and blankets as the dark nights draw in. A grandmother teaches her grandchildren to carve a neep lantern, keeping the old ways alive. Someone who usually feels out of place finds that, for one night, the whole world looks a little more like them. And some, more seriously, light a candle in the window for a loved one they have lost.

I understand why such evenings appeal. In a world of constant bad news, with families and communities breaking down, any reason to get together seems welcome. Many find a real sense of bonding in facing fears and laughing at them together, with black humour and corny, tongue-in-cheek horror. Many also use the occasion to raise money for charity, and that generosity is a good thing in itself. Most people join in for fun and mean no harm. And many of the things people are looking for (love for their children, belonging, community, comfort, remembrance of those they have lost) are good things, which God Himself has put in our hearts.

So the question is not whether those longings are good. It is whether this night can truly satisfy them, and what it is teaching them while they enjoy it.

## Where Halloween came from

To answer that, it helps first to know what the night actually is. The name means “All Hallows’ Even”, the eve of All Saints’ Day, a feast set by the Church of Rome in the Middle Ages. Its customs grew up around praying for the dead, in the belief that such prayers could help their souls on into heaven, which the Bible shows to be in vain. “Trick or treat” comes from “souling”, when the poor went door to door begging cakes in return for prayers for the dead. Behind that lies an older pagan festival at the start of winter, long associated with spirits and fortune-telling.

For those who treasure Scottish tradition, there is a part of Scotland’s story worth knowing. Guising and lanterns are often cherished as “the old ways”, but they grew up around a night of wandering spirits and prayers for the dead. At the Reformation, Scotland abolished such days, because none of it came from the Bible. The Scottish Reformers did not do this to rob anyone of joy, but because they had rediscovered something far better in the Scriptures, and many who came after them counted it worth their lives. To set Halloween aside is not to abandon Scotland’s heritage; it is to return to the noblest part of it.

## What is really being celebrated?

Some will say, “We don’t care about the history; it’s just fun now. We’re not celebrating anything dark. We’re celebrating our children, our friends, our street.” I believe them. But the things people celebrate *with* still carry their own message, whatever they intend. A child who dresses as a ghost, or knocks on doors between skeletons and witches, is quietly being told that the dead may wander among us, that witchcraft is a game, and that death itself is something to laugh at. These are not harmless ideas, as we shall see, for they contradict what God has told us about death and the world of spirits. The imagery of the night is violence, death, witchcraft and fear, turned into entertainment, and imagery teaches, even when nobody means it to.

And enjoying something does not make it good. The Bible speaks of “the pleasures of sin for a season” (Hebrews 11:25), and warns, “There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof *are* the ways of death” (Proverbs 14:12). In the end, what matters is not how a thing feels to us, but how God sees it.

## Why the Bible should decide

But how can we know how God sees it? I have already begun quoting the Bible as though its word settles the matter, and you may fairly ask why it should. The answer is that from beginning to end it claims to be the Word of God. “Thus saith the LORD” runs through it hundreds of times, and it declares that “All scripture *is* given by inspiration of God” (2 Timothy 3:16). It also proves that claim in a way no other book can: by foretelling the future. God Himself sets this test: “*I am* God, and *there is* none like me, Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times *the things* that are not *yet* done” (Isaiah 46:9-10). For example, centuries in advance, the prophets foretold that the Saviour would be born in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2); that He would be despised and rejected, wounded for the sins of others, and laid in a rich man’s grave (Isaiah 53); and that men would cast lots for His clothing (Psalm 22:18). All of this was fulfilled in Jesus Christ.

Isaiah and Micah both prophesied around 700 B.C., some seven centuries before Christ. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls is a complete copy of Isaiah, made over a century before Jesus was born. It agrees closely with the Bibles we read today, showing both that these words were written beforehand and that they have been faithfully preserved.

Prophecy is only one of many such evidences. Consider how the Bible came to be: some forty writers, among them kings, shepherds, fishermen and a physician, writing over some fifteen centuries, in three languages and in every kind of circumstance. Yet from beginning to end it speaks with one voice, without contradicting itself. No human committee could have planned such a book, still less carried it through across so many generations. There is much, much more that could be said about how the Bible proves itself to be the Word of God; yet in the end it is God Himself who persuades the heart, by His Spirit working through His Word. Read it for yourself (the Gospel of John is a good place to begin), and see whether it speaks as no other book does.

## Why do people love to be frightened?

Why do so many enjoy being scared? Why do people laugh hardest at the things they fear most? Perhaps a fright that seems harmless lets people brush against the thing they fear most of all, death, while feeling safe. Scripture calls death “the king of terrors” (Job 18:14). The film ends, the lights come on, and all is well. But death does not end when the film does. The Bible speaks of those “who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage” (Hebrews 2:15). Halloween does not free anyone from that fear; it only lets people laugh at it for a night, and then leaves it exactly where it was.

Sadly, our deadly enemy the devil knows this very well, and uses it to draw people, especially children and teenagers, further into his soul-destroying ways. There are real, dark and evil spiritual forces at work in our world, and our own neighbourhoods are no exception. Many a teenager has sat round a Ouija board at a Halloween sleepover, half laughing, half daring. It is not a harmless game. It is a way of asking spirits to speak, and whatever answers back is not a friend. God says, “There shall not be found among you *any one* […] that useth divination, *or* an observer of times, or an enchanter, or a witch, Or a charmer, or a consulter with familiar spirits, or a wizard, or a necromancer. For all that do these things *are* an abomination unto the LORD […]” (Deuteronomy 18:10-12).

Many who were once deeply involved in witchcraft and spiritualism, and have since been set free by Christ, warn against Halloween for this very reason. They do not see it as harmless, but as an open door, a season when tarot cards, séances and Ouija boards are passed into ordinary hands, and when curiosity can lead step by step into a darkness far deeper than anyone intended. Many of them speak of the torment and harm it brought into their lives and homes, and of the great joy and thankfulness they now know in being delivered from it, for God “hath delivered us from the power of darkness, and hath translated *us* into the kingdom of his dear Son” (Colossians 1:13).

Some would say that cartoon ghosts and jokey witches simply make fun of evil. But the night does two things at once: it misrepresents the spirit world, and it trivialises what is real in it.

## What the night gets wrong

First, it misrepresents. RE:Online, a guide to world religions for teachers in UK schools, says that at this time “Communicating with ancestors and departed loved ones is easy”.[^1] The “ghosts” of Halloween teach the same falsehood, for the Bible tells us that at death the soul goes at once to heaven or to hell (Luke 16:22-23; Luke 23:43).

The Bible records only one occasion when a departed soul appeared to someone consulting a medium: Samuel, to King Saul (1 Samuel 28). Commentators differ on whether it was Samuel himself or an evil spirit impersonating him, but either way Saul died for “asking *counsel* of *one that had* a familiar spirit” (1 Chronicles 10:13). Scripture warns again and again against such “familiar spirits” (Leviticus 19:31). They are not departed souls, but fallen angels, malevolent and intent on our eternal destruction, who mislead and lie to those who seek their counsel, for “Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14).

To misrepresent the spirit world is no small matter. It grieves God, whose Word it contradicts, and it is deadly dangerous for adults and children alike, for it leads them away from the saving truth of His Word and into the lies of the devil, who “is a liar, and the father of it” (John 8:44). And his lies come sugar-coated, like the sweets handed out at the door.

Worst of all, if the dead linger among us, then death is not the end of our opportunity, and there is always time later to set things right with God. But Scripture says otherwise: at death the soul goes at once to heaven or to hell, as the rich man and Lazarus did (Luke 16:22-23); there is “a great gulf fixed” between the saved and the lost (Luke 16:26), and “after this the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27). That is why God says, “behold, now *is* the accepted time; behold, now *is* the day of salvation” (2 Corinthians 6:2). And again, “Boast not thyself of to morrow; for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth” (Proverbs 27:1).

## If you miss someone you have lost

Perhaps you are reading this having set a place at the table, or lit a candle in the window, for someone you loved and lost. I do not write to mock that. The ache of missing someone is real, and the longing to be near them again is one of the deepest things in the human heart.

But I must tell you honestly what God’s Word says, because it matters too much to soften. Those we have lost cannot come back to us, and the spirits that seem to answer are not them. The rich man in the Lord’s parable could not cross back, nor could Lazarus be sent (Luke 16:26-31). Yet the Bible does not leave us without hope in grief, though it is a hope only for those who die trusting in Christ. To believers it says, “I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him” (1 Thessalonians 4:13-14). And the Lord Jesus said, beside the grave of a friend He loved, “I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live” (John 11:25).

No one can see into the heart of someone who has died; that rests with God alone, who judges righteously. But you can know where you stand with Him now, while there is still time.

A candle in the window cannot guide anyone home. But Christ is “the light of the world” (John 8:12), and He is the only way home to God for the living.

## What the night makes light of

Second, Halloween trivialises what is real, such as witchcraft, which Scripture treats as deadly serious. Those who practise witchcraft today do not treat this night as a joke: for modern witches and pagans it is one of the most sacred nights of the year, and the Church of Satan names it among its major festivals.[^2] If those who practise these things take them so seriously, is it wise for anyone else to treat them lightly? How can we know where harmless fun ends? God has given the answer: “Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. Abstain from all appearance of evil” (1 Thessalonians 5:21-22).

## The fountain of joy

Perhaps by now you are wondering whether God is simply against enjoyment. The truth is the very opposite. “God is love” (1 John 4:16); He is “the blessed God” (1 Timothy 1:11), perfect in joy in Himself from all eternity to all eternity, the everlasting fountain of all true joy: “in thy presence *is* fulness of joy; at thy right hand *there are* pleasures for evermore” (Psalm 16:11). He is “the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy” (1 Timothy 6:17). Every good pleasure anyone has ever known flows from Him. He is the Creator of laughter itself, and His Word tells us there is “a time to laugh” (Ecclesiastes 3:4). When He gave Sarah a son in her old age, her joy overflowed: “God hath made me to laugh, *so that* all that hear will laugh with me” (Genesis 21:6). And in the Lord’s parable of the prodigal son, the son’s homecoming was celebrated with music and dancing (Luke 15:25). And it records the joy of His people when He brought them home from captivity in Babylon: “Then was our mouth filled with laughter, and our tongue with singing: then said they among the heathen, The LORD hath done great things for them” (Psalm 126:2).

We may, and should, hold joyful celebrations on proper occasions. But surely our celebrations should never lead anyone, least of all children, towards the very things God warns us against.

## The bad news

Death is no game: “it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment” (Hebrews 9:27). God, our faithful Creator, forbids dealings with spirits and fortune-telling (Deuteronomy 18:10-12) because these things belong to a darkness that holds every one of us by nature. God made the first man in His own image, upright and holy (Genesis 1:27; Ecclesiastes 7:29). But he listened to the devil and fell, and the whole human race fell with him; that is why the world is so full of bad news. Our very nature is now corrupted, so that we sin readily and willingly. Jesus said, “Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin” (John 8:34), and the Bible describes all who are without Christ as caught in “the snare of the devil, who are taken captive by him at his will” (2 Timothy 2:26).

Many kind and generous people would say, “But I’m a decent person. I help my neighbours and give to charity.” Such kindness is good, and the world would be poorer without it. But it cannot wash away sin. For what is sin? “Sin is the transgression of the law” (1 John 3:4): the breaking of God’s holy law, summarised in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1-17). Who has never put something else before God, taken His name in vain, lied, stolen, or coveted what belongs to another? And the Lord Jesus taught that God’s law reaches even to the thoughts and desires of the heart (Matthew 5:21-22). Measured against God’s perfect holiness, “we are all as an unclean *thing*, and all our righteousnesses *are* as filthy rags” (Isaiah 64:6). We cannot pay off a debt to God with good deeds, any more than a man can be acquitted of a crime because he was kind to others on other days.

How marvellous is God’s patience with sinners! Day after day He bears with their sin, makes His sun rise on the evil and the good (Matthew 5:45), and holds back the judgment that sin deserves. Yet that patience must never be presumed upon: “Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance?” (Romans 2:4). For God is also holy and just. Think of a judge who let a man guilty of a wicked crime walk free, simply because he said he was sorry, or because the judge felt kindly towards him: we would not call him a good judge, but a corrupt one. God is the perfectly good Judge: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?” (Genesis 18:25). And so in the end He must and will punish sin.

Nor is any sin a small thing. A crime is weighed by the one against whom it is committed: to strike a stranger is serious, but to strike the king is treason. Every sin is committed against the infinitely great and holy God, and so even a single sin is infinitely serious. “For whosoever shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one *point*, he is guilty of all” (James 2:10). That is why the punishment matches the crime, and why His just sentence on all who die in their sins is eternal damnation, for of them the Lord Jesus says, “these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal” (Matthew 25:46).

## The good news

But God has the best possible news for us (the very word “gospel” means “good news”). In His mercy, grace and love, He has not left us there. He seeks to rescue us from sin, from Satan, and from the judgment to come.

That is why Jesus Christ came, “that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil; And deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage” (Hebrews 2:14-15). Here is the real answer to the fear of death: not to laugh at it for a night, but to be set free from it for ever. He died for sinners and rose again. On the cross He bore, in the place of all who trust in Him, the punishment their sins deserve, so that God can pardon them without ceasing to be a just Judge: He is “just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus” (Romans 3:26). He says, “I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life” (John 8:12). He alone can bring us out of that darkness. Against all the devil’s lies, He declares, “I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me” (John 14:6). As the apostle Peter preached, “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved” (Acts 4:12).

This salvation is not earned; it is a gift. “For the wages of sin *is* death; but the gift of God *is* eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord” (Romans 6:23).

And this gift is offered freely to all who will come. The Lord Jesus loves children. When His disciples tried to turn them away, He said, “Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God” (Mark 10:14). If you love your children and want them to have the happiest memories, He wants something better still for them: that they should know Him.

Nor does He turn away those who feel they do not fit in. The respectable people of His day complained, “This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them” (Luke 15:2). Those who are made to feel strange, unwanted or out of place find in Him a welcome that does not last for one night only, but for ever. And of those who hear, believe and trust in Him, He promises, “The LORD thy God in the midst of thee *is* mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing” (Zephaniah 3:17).

Turn from your sin and trust in Christ, for He promises, “him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out” (John 6:37). “Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved” (Acts 16:31).

## A light in the darkness

As the dark nights draw in, it is worth pausing over a few questions. What do you most hope your children will remember? What are you really looking for when you gather with friends on a cold night? Why do people laugh hardest at what they fear most? And when your own life is over, where will you be?

Halloween gives people a reason to gather, a few hours of thrills, and a pumpkin’s flickering light against the dark. But God offers something far better: peace with Him through Jesus Christ, freedom from the fear of death, a welcome that never ends, and the joys of His presence for ever, in a new world free from sin and loneliness. Of all who are His, it is written, “God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain” (Revelation 21:4). And of that world we read, “there shall be no night there; and they need no candle, neither light of the sun; for the Lord God giveth them light” (Revelation 22:5).

“The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light” (Isaiah 9:2). That light still shines, and it is shining for you.
